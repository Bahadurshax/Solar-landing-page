Partners, five ways

Reading this as: a new section inside an existing, documented brand system, for homeowners and small commercial buyers who want to know whose equipment ends up on their roof. Language is the page's own: warm near-monochrome, one accent, two weights of one typeface. Leaning toward native CSS in the section-stylesheet pattern the repo already uses, not a new dependency.

This is a preserve-mode addition rather than a greenfield one, so every variant below is built from the Eclipse tokens, the Albert Sans ramp, and the section rhythm in DESIGN.md. None of them introduces a colour, a face, a radius or a breakpoint the page does not already have. They differ by composition, which is the only variable actually worth testing.

  • Variance 7
  • Motion 6
  • Density 3
  • Mode Redesign, preserve

Placeholder data. The six partners below are invented, and their marks are drawn rather than fetched. Putting a real manufacturer's logo on this page would claim a supply relationship that does not exist yet. Swap the names and drop in real SVGs when there are real ones to name; nothing in the layouts depends on the placeholder content.

Two house rules shaped these more than any aesthetic preference. The Brand Face Rule means no partner name is ever set in Unbounded, so none of the five reaches for a second typeface to make a name feel important. And DESIGN.md rations the page to three scroll-pinned sequences, which is why variant 04 is a free-scrolling rail rather than the horizontal hijack this content would otherwise invite.

01

Ledger

The anti-logo-wall. Partners are set as an index at title scale, with what they supply held out to the right, and the reader gets names rather than a grid of trademarks to squint at. It is the most direct expression of the page's stated belief that a specific fact outperforms a superlative.

Pick this if the partner names carry weight on their own, or if you would rather not chase logo permissions.

  • Hour: night
  • Family: editorial index
  • Motion: hairline draw on hover
  • Risk: reads plain without strong names

02

Drift

One band, moving slowly, bled past both gutters and faded at the edges so it reads as a continuing line rather than a row that happens to be wide. The motion is doing one job: saying there are more of these than the frame can hold. It pauses on hover so anyone who wants to actually read a name can.

Pick this if the section should cost the page almost nothing, and breadth matters more than detail. Cheapest of the five in vertical space by a wide margin.

The objection, and it is a fair one: a band that moves on its own asks for attention it has not earned, and half its content is off screen at any moment. It pauses on hover and stops dead under reduced motion, but a reader who never hovers never sees the full list. If this one is chosen, that trade is the thing to settle first.

  • Hour: midday
  • Family: marquee band
  • Motion: continuous, pausable
  • Risk: only one marquee per page

03

Plates

Six partners, six cells, no filler tile. Two wide plates carry real photographs of the hardware and four narrow ones carry a name, so the grid has a rhythm instead of being six identical boxes. It is the only variant that shows the equipment rather than just naming who made it.

Pick this if the section needs to carry visual weight, or if it is going to sit between two type-heavy sections that need breaking up.

  • Hour: dusk
  • Family: bento grid
  • Motion: slow scale on hover
  • Risk: needs real photography

04

Rail

A lateral read that does not take the scroll away from anyone. Cards snap, the rail bleeds off the right edge so it is visibly continuable, and the section holds a fixed height no matter how many partners are added later. The one variant that scales past six without a redesign.

Pick this if the list is going to grow, or if the page needs a horizontal moment but cannot afford a fourth pinned sequence.

  • Hour: dawn
  • Family: snap rail
  • Motion: lift on hover, native scroll
  • Risk: right-edge content is missable

05

Field

The suppliers placed on an open field and joined by a drawn hairline, which is the same device the region map already uses for routes. That is the argument for it: a supply chain and a service area are the same kind of drawing, so this variant reads as something the page already knew how to do rather than as a new idea bolted on.

Pick this if you want the section to feel authored. It is the highest-variance option here and the one most likely to look like a diagram if the lines are turned up even slightly.

  • Hour: late afternoon
  • Family: constellation
  • Motion: line draws on entry
  • Risk: closest to decoration
